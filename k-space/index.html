k-space

An MR scanner never takes a picture. Its gradients step through spatial frequencies and the coil records how much of each one the body contains: the image's Fourier transform, called k-space. The image is computed afterwards, with an inverse FFT. What you sample, and how well, decides what the image looks like.

How to read k-space

Every point is a wave. A point at (kx, ky) stands for a stripe pattern across the whole image, with a spacing of 1/|k| and pointing along k. Its value says how much of that pattern the image contains, and with which phase. Click anywhere in the k-space panel to see the stripe a point represents.

The centre holds contrast, the edges hold detail. The low frequencies near the centre carry the overall brightness of each tissue; the high frequencies at the edges carry edges and fine structure. Keep only the centre and you get the right contrast, blurred; keep only the edges and you get an outline.

Spacing sets the field of view, extent sets resolution. The step between sampled lines, Δk, fixes the field of view at 1/Δk: skip every other line and the field of view halves, so anything outside it folds back over the image (aliasing). The highest frequency sampled, kmax, fixes the smallest detail at about 1/(2kmax).

Time is lines. Each phase-encoding line takes one repetition, so scan time grows with the number of lines. Every acceleration technique, from partial Fourier to parallel imaging, compressed sensing and deep-learning reconstruction, is a way to acquire fewer lines and fill in what is missing.
