CT reconstruction

A CT scanner sends X-rays through the body from hundreds of angles and measures how much gets through. Each measurement is the total attenuation along one line. Stacked by angle, they form the sinogram; the image is recovered by smearing each projection back across the slice, after a filter that undoes the blur this smearing creates.

Reading it

Projections. An X-ray beam of intensity I₀ leaves the body at I = I₀ e−∫μ dl, so −ln(I/I₀) is the line integral of the attenuation coefficient μ along the ray: the Radon transform. A point in the body traces a sine wave across the sinogram as the gantry turns, which gives the sinogram its name.

Why the filter. Spreading each projection back along its rays puts every point's value in the right place, but also smears it over the whole image, giving a blur that falls off as 1/r. In frequency terms, back-projection over-weights low frequencies by 1/|f|; multiplying each projection by |f|, the ramp filter, cancels it exactly (the Fourier slice theorem). The ramp also amplifies high-frequency noise, so windows such as Shepp-Logan or Hann roll it off, trading a little sharpness for less noise.

Views and dose. Too few angles leave streaks radiating from edges. Fewer photons, a lower dose, means each measurement is noisier: the noise in the image grows as the square root of the dose falls, so halving the noise takes four times the radiation. Iterative and deep-learning reconstructions in modern scanners are, above all, ways of getting diagnostic images from fewer photons.

Hounsfield units. CT images are reported as HU = 1000 × (μ − μwater) / μwater: water is 0, air −1000, fat about −100, brain 20 to 40, bone up to +1000 and beyond. Brain tissues differ by only tens of HU, which is why a narrow display window is used to read them.
