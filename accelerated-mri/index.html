Accelerated MRI

Scan time is lines of k-space. Compressed sensing acquires only a fraction of them, at random, and then searches for the image that agrees with what was measured and is as simple as possible in a wavelet basis. Medical images are compressible, so that simplest image is usually the right one.

Why it works

Images are sparse in the right basis. A wavelet transform describes an image by its coarse shape plus local details at several scales. For anatomy, most detail coefficients are close to zero, so a few percent of them hold almost all the information, which is also how JPEG 2000 compresses photos.

Random sampling makes the artefact look like noise. Skipping lines on a regular grid folds the image onto itself: a coherent copy that a sparsity prior cannot tell apart from real anatomy. Skipping them at random spreads the error as low-level, noise-like interference across the image, which thresholding the wavelet coefficients removes. Switch the pattern to regular and the same algorithm fails.

The algorithm. FISTA alternates two steps: a gradient step that pulls the estimate towards consistency with the acquired k-space lines, and a soft threshold that shrinks small wavelet coefficients to zero. λ sets the balance: too small and the aliasing stays, too large and real detail is erased. Noise in the data calls for a larger λ.

x̂ = argminx ‖M F x − y‖² + λ ‖W x‖₁ (M: sampled lines, F: Fourier transform, W: wavelets)

Compressed sensing reached clinical scanners in 2017; today deep-learning reconstructions, trained on datasets such as fastMRI, learn the prior from data instead of assuming wavelet sparsity, and allow even larger accelerations.
