Image analysis

A reconstructed image is not yet an answer. A radiologist, or software, has to clean it, find the brain, label each tissue and measure what matters, such as the size of a lesion. Here the head phantom is scanned with three contrasts, noise is added, and a simple pipeline segments it. Because the phantom's true anatomy is known, every step can be scored.

Reading it

Noise in MR magnitude images is Rician. The scanner measures a complex signal with Gaussian noise in both parts; taking the magnitude turns it into a Rician distribution, which is no longer zero-mean: dark regions such as air come out at about 1.25σ instead of 0. That bias matters when measuring low signals.

Denoising trades noise for blur. A Gaussian filter averages neighbours and softens every edge; a median filter takes the middle value of a window, which removes outliers while keeping boundaries. Modern pipelines use non-local means, total variation or neural networks, all built on the same trade-off.

Skull stripping, then classification. Otsu's method picks the threshold that best separates a histogram into two groups, here head and background (the skull, with almost no signal, falls out with the air). Inside the mask, k-means groups pixels by their intensities. With one contrast, grey matter and lesions overlap; adding T2 and FLAIR separates them, which is why clinical protocols acquire several contrasts.

Partial volume. Even with no noise the score is not perfect: pixels on a boundary contain two tissues and get a mixed signal. Thin structures such as the cortical ribbon suffer most, and a boundary between a bright and a dark tissue can mimic a third one in between: here the edge of the scalp passes for lesion until a clean-up step removes most of the stray pixels. Real segmentation tools (FreeSurfer, FSL FAST, SPM, and deep networks such as nnU-Net) model partial volume, intensity inhomogeneity and anatomical priors on top of this basic idea.
